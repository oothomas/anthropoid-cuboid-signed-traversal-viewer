Dense correspondence and interpretable skeletal morphospaces

Comparative skeletal biology seeks to relate anatomical variation to function and evolutionary history. The primate cuboid, a bone of the lateral midfoot, presents a demanding case: its joint surfaces, plantar tuberosity and peroneus longus tendon groove form a complex anatomy relevant to foot mechanics. [1] [2] Comparing this anatomy requires measuring differences across curved surfaces, articular margins and local relief, not only at isolated points.

The methodological gap

Three-dimensional scans capture detailed surfaces, but do not establish which locations correspond between specimens. Anatomical landmarks provide expert-defined anchors, yet may undersample variation between them. [3] Earlier work, including morphVQ, brought learned descriptors and functional maps into biological morphometrics. [4] This study connects anatomically constrained dense matching to a reconstruction-capable morphospace, so that statistical variation can be examined as changes in surface anatomy.

A two-stage approach

Stage 1 builds dense correspondences across a sparse network of specimens and templates. Panine et al.'s method uses corresponding anatomical landmarks to construct spectral bases adapted to those anchors. It matches surface functions in these bases and iteratively refines dense point-to-point correspondences, accommodating differences in shape and proportions. [5] [6] Correspondence is a methodological component in its own right because matching errors propagate into shape analysis.

Stage 2 uses functional-map networks and a limit-shape common basis to make surface features comparable across specimens. [7] [8] The autoencoder builds on DISCO-AE, with DiffusionNet surface layers and an SNK-inspired prism decoder adapted for reconstruction from a common latent code. [9] [10] [11] Principal-component analysis (PCA) summarizes variation in those codes. Decoding along the axes reveals associated anatomical changes, linking statistical relationships among specimens to surface geometry.

Study scope and initial results

Anthropoid cuboids are the primary application. A non-fossil hominoid subset examines the same element within a narrower taxonomic range; mouse mandibles and humeri test the approach on different skeletal geometries. The current models produce reconstructed surfaces and decoded shape variation for all four datasets. These outputs make local contours, projections and depressions available for anatomical inspection alongside specimen relationships in the morphospace.

Earlier development analyses found agreement between learned shape relationships and manual-landmark and automated pseudolandmark morphometrics, with decoded cuboid axes localizing articular and plantar variation. These findings concern earlier model versions. Reconstruction, morphometric agreement, repeatability, phylogenetic signal and size associations are being reassessed for the revised datasets and models shown here.

Biological significance

The contribution is a connection between whole-surface measurement and anatomical explanation, complementing established geometric morphometrics. Instead of stopping at separation in a scatterplot, researchers can examine where and how surfaces differ and formulate explicit hypotheses about joint form, tendon pathways and skeletal diversity. Such hypotheses still require comparative or biomechanical tests: decoded variation alone does not establish locomotor adaptation or evolutionary causation.

Source software and methodological credit

These upstream projects underpin the methods and implementation. Our biological adaptations, quality controls and results are the responsibility of this study.

  • Panine landmark-adapted matching

    Stage 1 non-isometric functional matching with anatomical landmark constraints. [6]

  • Surface Maps via Adaptive Triangulations

    Foundation for the revised mandible correspondence branch, with project-specific initialization, anchor, solver and export safeguards. [12]

  • DISCO-AE

    Common-basis autoencoding framework underlying Stage 2. [9]

  • DiffusionNet

    Surface-network layers used in the encoder and decoder. [10]

  • SNK

    Prism-decoder design adapted within our common-basis autoencoder, not an application of SNK's pairwise zero-shot matching procedure. [11]

  • Robin Magnet's pyFM

    Mesh, spectral and functional-map-network utilities used by the pipeline. Cited directly as software, following the author's attribution guidance. [13]

Dataset notes and provenance

Interactive principal-component traversals of mouse mandibles. The seed-2071 model at epoch 350 is decoded on fixed template 802_13. PCA is fitted to 93 training specimen codes; 0 tuning codes are projected. Panels PC1-PC4 each contain 41 actual decoded positions from -2 to +2 training-score SD. Blue indicates inward and orange/red outward displacement along the decoded-mean normal; white is zero. Values are percent of the mean bounding-box diagonal, not reconstruction error or unmapped coverage; tangential motion is not encoded. The symmetric display limit clips the outer 2% of magnitudes. 0 of 164 positions have geometry flags; counts are per sampled surface, not a guarantee for a continuous path or anatomical validity. Templates and confirmation codes are excluded from PCA fitting and score plots. Axes are within-model, not aligned between seeds or datasets. Anatomical interpretation remains provisional.
